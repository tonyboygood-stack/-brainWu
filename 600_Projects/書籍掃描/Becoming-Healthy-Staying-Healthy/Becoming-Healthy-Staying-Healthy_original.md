1. Tune to the Season and to Time 
We’ve built our lives often far from our warm, subtropical 
origins. We invented electric lights to stay awake in the 
dark, and heavy curtains to stay asleep past dawn. We 
heat our homes in winter and cool them in summer. 
As modern as we are, deep within we are still following 
natural seasonal rhythms. Comfort is important, but if 
we overcool or overheat our living spaces, the body is 
not able to adjust to the current season, or to the time 
of day. Te body is obstructed from going through its 
natural cycles of storing fat for winter, or generating 
excitement for spring, for example. If we over-illuminate 
our living space at night, we limit the hormonal shifts 
the body needs to make as the sun goes down. Tis 
makes it diﬃcult to fall into a deep sleep easily and with 
consistency. 
During the winter, our bodies go into conservation 
mode. In Chinese medicine, this is shown in the pulses 
as they sink noticeably deeper in the wrist—the body 
is conserving energy for spring and summer. Being 
quiet at night in winter will honor this natural process 
and enable you to feel more harmonious. During the 
evenings, go with the season and practice enjoying the 
substantially slower pace that winter invites, rather than

feeling disappointed. To ensure hormonal changes occur 
in balance, minimize the ambient light in your home and 
make sure that there is at least one dark corner in the 
room. Tis signals to the body that the sun has set and 
that we should prepare for rest. Sleep in turn deepens, 
and the regulation of digestion, hormones, and mental

Bright lights late in the day during winter, especially

In spring and summer it is desirable to go to bed later and 
to get up earlier as we naturally open up more energy; 
the days are longer, food is fresh and ready for harvest, 
and it’s beautiful to be out in the sun.

it. Te manner in which you wake is important. Waking

Ann Cecil-Sterman is the author of the widely acclaimed books: 
Te Art of Pulse Diagnosis, Illustrations of the Complete Acupuncture 
System, Tongue Diagnosis, and Advanced Acupuncture: A Clinic 
Manual, a required text in many acupuncture schools. Currently 
she teaches a six month training and mentorship immersion 
program to students on ﬁve continents every year. She also travels 
extensively to reach students in person for reﬁning practice of the 
Complement Channels, pulse diagnosis, and with her husband, 
Andrew Sterman, the use of food as medicine. For many years 
she taught Advanced Clinical Observation and was a senior clinic 
supervisor at the school of acupuncture in New York founded in 
1997 by the great master Jeﬀrey Yuen, who was her teacher for 
over twenty years. Her patients—children and adults of all ages—
have come from all over the world to work through illnesses or 
on personal cultivation. She lives in Manhattan with her family. 
www.anncecilsterman.com
About the Photographer
Ravi Zohar Sterman is a photographer and artist. He studied at the 
Pratt Institute in New York. His photographs have appeared at the 
Metropolitan Museum of Art and in various magazines.
About the Designer
Cody Dodo studied acupuncture under Master Jeﬀrey Yuen at the 
Swedish Institute in New York and later completed advanced studies 
with him in acupuncture and Chinese dietary therapy. Prior to his 
pursuit of acupuncture, Cody had a long career as a graphic designer 
in the publishing industry. Cody teaches Classical Acupuncture 
internationally, and is dedicated to the transmission of this wonderful 
medicine. He also designed the author’s other books, Advanced 
Acupuncture: A Clinic Manual, Illustrations of the Complete Acupuncture 
System, Te Art of Pulse Diagnosis and Tongue Diagnosis.
